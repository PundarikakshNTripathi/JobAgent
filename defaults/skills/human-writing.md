# Writing messages that read like a person, not a model

Use this for every LinkedIn note, DM, email, follow-up, cover letter and free-text answer.

## What a good cold message from a student looks like
- 2 to 5 short sentences. Plain words. Written the way the person would actually type it on their phone after thinking for a minute.
- Open on ONE specific thing about the reader that is checkable (a repo, a talk, a post, their team's work), stated flatly. Not praised, just named.
- One sentence on who the writer is, with one concrete fact (a number, a project, a repo). Not a list of credentials.
- One small, specific question at the end. Easy to answer in one reply. No "would love to connect", no "any advice".
- Sign with the first name only (or nothing on LinkedIn).
- It is fine to be a little plain or slightly blunt. Real messages are not polished.

## Tells that make a message read as machine-written. Never use them.
- Greeting fluff: "I hope this finds you well", "Hope you're doing well", "I came across your profile", "I am writing to", "I wanted to reach out", "reaching out".
- Flattery and filler adjectives: impressive, fascinating, inspiring, remarkable, exciting, passionate, thrilled, excited, eager, keen, love, resonates, "huge fan".
- Stock verbs and phrases: leverage, delve, showcase, align, at the intersection of, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, game-changing, robust, seamless, journey, tapestry, testament, "pick your brain", "a quick chat", "would love to", "I'd be grateful", "kindly", "in today's".
- Em dashes and en dashes used as punctuation. Use a comma or a full stop instead.
- Three-item lists ("X, Y, and Z") and parallel "not just A but B" constructions.
- Semicolons, bullet points, bold, headings, emoji, exclamation marks.
- Throat-clearing openers and closers: "Additionally", "Furthermore", "Moreover", "In conclusion", "Thanks in advance", "Looking forward to hearing from you", "Best regards".
- Restating the reader's bio back to them. Explaining why they are a great fit. Over-apologising ("sorry to bother you").
- Starting every sentence with "I".

## Do
- Use contractions (I'm, it's, don't). Mix a short sentence with a longer one.
- Say the concrete thing instead of the category: "a ternary matmul kernel, 5.6x over a naive GPU baseline" beats "extensive experience in low-level optimization".
- Be honest about the gap in one clause when it matters ("no Triton yet"), without grovelling.
- Ask for what you actually want: first messages ask a question or for a pointer, not a referral. Ask for the referral only to someone who already replied, or who is clearly the right person and a first-degree connection.
- Match the reader's likely register: relaxed for engineers and researchers, a bit more formal for recruiters.

## Cover letters and free-text answers
- Same rules. First sentence states something specific about the team or problem, not "I am excited to apply".
- No "I believe I would be a great fit". Show the one or two pieces of evidence and stop.
- Under 200 words. No headings. No bullet points unless the form field asks for a list.

## Good vs bad (same facts)
BAD: "Hi Priya, I hope this message finds you well! I came across your impressive work on kernels at Acme and I'm truly passionate about low-level optimization. I'd love to connect and pick your brain about the Kernel Engineer role. Thanks in advance!"
GOOD: "Hi Priya, saw you're on the kernel team at Acme. I'm a final-year student, been writing CUDA kernels on my own (a matmul kernel, 5.6x over a naive GPU baseline). I'm applying to the new-grad kernel role. What does the team screen for first in new grads?"
